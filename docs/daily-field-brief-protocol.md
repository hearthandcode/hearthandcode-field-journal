# Daily Field Brief: Public-Projection Protocol

This document defines the Field Brief as a public content form within the Hearth & Code Field Journal. It sits alongside three other intentionally distinct content forms: the essay, the design record, and the build receipt. Together these four forms constitute the public surface of the Field Journal. This protocol is a companion to the Field Journal Editorial Contract (`docs/editorial-contract.md`) and inherits its source-boundary rules, claim labeling system, and human-review gate.

---

## 1. What a Field Brief Is

A Field Brief is a deliberately small, optional public projection of a single research moment. It captures one concrete observation, change, or question from active work and makes it legible to an outside reader.

A Field Brief is:

- **A snapshot**: one bounded observation from a single working session or research activity.
- **Optional**: no schedule governs publication. A Field Brief publishes when Scott selects a moment worth projecting, never on a calendar.
- **Curated**: the moment is chosen by a human, not extracted or generated by automation.
- **Reviewed**: every Field Brief passes the human review gate described in the editorial contract before it reaches `status: published`.

A Field Brief is not:

- **Not a transcript**: it never reproduces raw conversation, session logs, or private notes.
- **Not a daily operational brief**: the private Hub produces its own internal daily record. The Field Brief is a separate, public-facing artifact with an entirely different audience and purpose.
- **Not a raw AI exchange**: AI may assist in drafting, but the selection of the moment, the framing, and the review are human decisions. Unedited AI output is never published.
- **Not an automated obligation**: no cron, scheduler, or agent produces a Field Brief. Publication is always an explicit human act.

## 2. The Four Content Forms

The Field Journal publishes four intentionally distinct content forms. Each has a different scope, audience, and structural convention.

### 2.1 Field Brief

| Attribute | Convention |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Project one research moment to the public surface. |
| **Scope** | One observation, change, or unresolved question. |
| **Typical length** | 100 to 400 words. |
| **Structure** | Date, heading, concrete observation, source or reproducible receipt (when applicable), E/I/P/Q label, one unresolved question, optional revision note. |
| **Frequency** | Irregular. A day with no Field Brief is normal. |
| **Examples** | "Today I noticed a pattern in how the code indexer resolves ambiguous symbols." "A build failed in an unexpected way and here is what the log revealed." |

### 2.2 Essay

| Attribute | Convention |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Develop a sustained argument, reflection, or synthesis. |
| **Scope** | A research theme, methodological question, or design philosophy. |
| **Typical length** | 800 to 2,500 words. |
| **Structure** | Narrative arc: problem, context, argument, evidence, reflection. |
| **Frequency** | Occasional. An essay is a considered piece, not a reaction. |
| **Examples** | "Why neurodivergent learners need cognitive scaffolding, not productivity frameworks." "Three patterns I observed across six months of building with language models." |

### 2.3 Design Record

| Attribute | Convention |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Document a design decision, its rationale, and its trade-offs. |
| **Scope** | One architectural or design choice, with context and alternatives considered. |
| **Typical length** | 500 to 1,500 words. |
| **Structure** | Context, decision, alternatives considered, consequences. |
| **Frequency** | As design decisions mature. Not every decision warrants a public record. |
| **Examples** | "Why the Field Journal uses Astro instead of a CMS." "The decision to separate the public Field Journal from the private Exocore Hub." |

### 2.4 Build Receipt

| Attribute | Convention |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Provide a reproducible record of a build, deployment, or configuration artifact. |
| **Scope** | One infrastructure or tooling artifact, with enough detail to reproduce. |
| **Typical length** | 300 to 800 words. |
| **Structure** | What was built, why, how to verify, artifact pointer. |
| **Frequency** | When a build result is worth sharing publicly. |
| **Examples** | "The Field Journal CI pipeline and how it deploys to Cloudflare Pages." "A reproducible Docker Compose stack for local Exocore development." |

These four forms coexist. A single week might produce two Field Briefs, one design record, and no essays. A month might produce an essay and several design records but no Field Briefs. The forms serve different purposes and are published on their own merits.

## 3. The Private-to-Public Path

Every Field Brief follows a defined path from private work to public artifact. No step is automated. Every step is a human decision or a human-reviewed draft.

### Step 1: Private Engagement or Capture

Work happens inside the private Exocore research program: coding sessions, research reading, architectural discussions, pilot assessments, methodology experiments. This work generates private artifacts: session logs, notes, specs, build logs, assessment records. These artifacts are governed by the editorial contract's source-boundary rules and are never published directly.

### Step 2: Human-Selected Artifact or Bounded Observation

Scott identifies a single moment from the work that is worth projecting publicly. The selection is a human judgment: is this observation interesting to an outside reader? Can it be framed without exposing prohibited source material? Is there a reproducible receipt (a public commit, a build log excerpt, a de-identified data point) that anchors the observation?

The selected moment is bounded: it is one observation, not a session summary. It is one result, not a research log.

### Step 3: AI-Assisted Draft

An AI drafting tool may produce the initial Field Brief text, drawing on the selected observation and any public or reviewed-internal sources. The AI-produced text is a draft. It is never published without human review.

### Step 4: Human Review for Voice, Privacy, and Claims

Scott reads the draft and checks three things:

- **Voice**: does the text sound like the Field Journal's editorial voice, or does it sound like an AI's default register?
- **Privacy**: does the text draw on any prohibited source class as defined in the editorial contract?
- **Claims**: does every substantive claim carry an E/I/P/Q label with a defensible basis?

If any of these checks fails, the draft is revised. The review gate is explicit: Scott marks the post `reviewGate: true` only when all three checks pass.

### Step 5: Explicit `status: published` Source Selection

When Scott is satisfied that the Field Brief is accurate, well-framed, and within the editorial boundary, he sets the frontmatter to `status: published` and adds a `published_date`. This is the final, irreversible step. A published Field Brief is visible on the public Field Journal site and archived in git history. Corrections use the amendment mechanism defined in the editorial contract.

## 4. The Smallest Field Brief Shape

A minimal Field Brief contains these elements, in this order:

1. **Date and heading**: the publication date and a sentence-case title describing the observation.
2. **One concrete change or observation**: a single finding, result, or pattern noticed during work.
3. **One source or reproducible receipt** (when applicable): a public commit hash, a de-identified data point, a neutral description of a reviewed internal artifact, or a public URL that anchors the observation. If the observation is purely reflective and no external source applies, this may be omitted.
4. **An E/I/P/Q label**: the claim label for the central observation, following the editorial contract's label system (`[evidence]`, `[inference]`, `[proposal]`, `[open-question]`).
5. **One unresolved question**: something the observation raises but does not answer.
6. **Optional revision note**: a brief note if the Field Brief revisits or updates a prior observation.

### Example Field Brief (Invented)

```markdown
---
title: "The indexer resolves ambiguous symbols 40 percent faster with a two-pass strategy"
date: 2026-08-01
labels: [evidence, open-question]
sources:
  - "Public commit: github.com/example/project/commit/abc123"
status: draft
pilot: false
reviewGate: false
---

## Observation

I replaced the single-pass symbol resolver in the code indexer with a two-pass strategy: first pass collects all candidates, second pass disambiguates using scope distance. The result surprised me.

**The change**: a single-pass resolver that made a best-guess assignment on first encounter was replaced with a collector pass followed by a scope-distance disambiguator. The test suite is a 200-module monorepo with deliberate naming collisions.

**The observation**: end-to-end indexing time dropped by roughly 40 percent on the collision-heavy test suite. The two-pass strategy produces fewer cache invalidations downstream because resolved symbols are stable.

**[evidence]** The commit `abc123` contains the implementation and the benchmark suite. Running `make bench-indexer` on that commit reproduces the 40 percent figure.

**Unresolved question**: the two-pass strategy's memory overhead scales linearly with candidate count. At what project size does the memory cost outweigh the indexing speed gain?

*Revision note: (none)*
```

### Example Field Brief (Minimal, Invented)

```markdown
---
title: "A textbook example of the frame problem in an agent loop"
date: 2026-08-03
labels: [inference, open-question]
sources:
  - "Reviewed internal research artifact: agent-loop debugging session, de-identified"
status: draft
pilot: false
reviewGate: false
---

## Observation

An agent tasked with refactoring a module kept re-reading the same three files on every turn, even after the files had not changed. The agent's context window grew until it hit the token limit and failed.

**[inference]** This looks like a textbook frame problem: the agent cannot distinguish relevant state changes from irrelevant ones, so it re-observes everything.

**Unresolved question**: would a simple file-modification timestamp check eliminate this behavior, or does the frame problem run deeper than file-level freshness?
```

Both examples use invented projects, invented commit hashes, and generic descriptions. They illustrate the shape without reproducing any real private material.

## 5. Skipping a Day (or a Week, or a Month)

The Field Brief has no schedule. There is no expectation that a Field Brief appears on any given day, or on any given cadence. A day with no Field Brief is not a gap or a failure; it is the default.

The Field Journal publishes when there is something to publish. The Field Brief, specifically, publishes when a single research moment is clear enough and interesting enough to be worth projecting. If no such moment presents itself, nothing is published. The silence is intentional: it means the work is happening, and the public projection threshold has not been met.

This protocol explicitly rejects the "daily blogging" convention. A daily obligation to publish produces filler. An optional, curated projection of real research moments produces signal.

## 6. Frontmatter and Schema

Field Briefs use the same frontmatter schema as all Field Journal posts, defined in the editorial contract (`docs/editorial-contract.md`, Section 7). The mapping to the Astro content collection at `src/content/posts/` is identical across all four content forms.

For a Field Brief, the following conventions apply beyond the required schema:

- `labels`: a Field Brief typically carries one or two labels. The central observation carries a label; the unresolved question carries `open-question`.
- `sources`: a Field Brief often has a single source: a public commit, a public URL, or a neutral reviewed-internal disclosure. Multiple sources are acceptable but rare given the scope.
- `pilot`: `false` unless the Field Brief is part of the launch pilot selection.
- `reviewGate`: `false` in draft; `true` only after Scott's explicit review.
- `status`: follows the editorial contract lifecycle: `draft` → `review` → `published`.

There is no `type` field in the frontmatter that distinguishes a Field Brief from an essay, design record, or build receipt. The distinction between the four content forms is structural and editorial, not a schema-level discriminant. The form is evident from the post's structure, not from a metadata tag.

## 7. Relationship to the Editorial Contract

This protocol is a subordinate document. The editorial contract governs all Field Journal content; this protocol describes the specific shape, conventions, and lifecycle of the Field Brief form. Where this protocol is silent, the editorial contract applies. Where a conflict exists, the editorial contract governs.

The editorial contract defines:

- Source classes: allowed and prohibited (Sections 2 and 3)
- Claim label system: E/I/P/Q (Section 4)
- Post lifecycle: draft, review, published (Section 5)
- Privacy exclusions (Section 6)
- Frontmatter requirements (Section 7)
- Human review gate (Section 9)
- Amendments and corrections (Section 10)

Every Field Brief obeys all of these rules. This protocol adds the Field Brief's specific shape and conventions on top of that shared foundation.

## 8. Governance

This protocol is maintained in the Field Journal repository at `docs/daily-field-brief-protocol.md`. Amendments follow the same lifecycle as posts: propose, review, publish. Scott is the reviewer.

The protocol is public. It is the reference document for anyone writing, reviewing, or reading a Field Brief.
